# Tacit Knowledge Transfer in Remote-First Organizations

## Abstract

Background: Tacit knowledge, as defined by Polanyi (1966) and
operationalized through Nonaka and Takeuchi's (1995) SECI model,
is fundamental to organizational competence. Remote-first work
arrangements interrupt the social and spatial contexts through which
tacit knowledge traditionally flows. Method: We develop a theoretical
framework analyzing how each phase of the SECI model is affected by
remote-first contexts, drawing on Theorem-005 (Tacit Knowledge
Inexpressibility) and the Organizational Amnesia Prevention Protocol
(RFC-0007). Key Finding: Remote-first contexts selectively impair
Socialization and Internalization while leaving Externalization and
Combination relatively intact, creating a systematic bias toward
explicit knowledge at the expense of embodied competence. Implication:
Organizations must design intentional proximate experiences to
compensate for the loss of ambient tacit knowledge transfer.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid shift to remote-first work arrangements has prompted
organizations to reexamine assumptions about how knowledge is
created, shared, and preserved. Much of this reexamination focuses
on explicit knowledge: documentation, procedures, decision logs.
But the deeper challenge lies in tacit knowledge, the embodied,
contextual, and often unconscious competence that distinguishes
expert performance from novice rule-following.

Polanyi (1966) articulated the core paradox: "We know more than
we can tell." Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) operationalized this
insight through the SECI model, which describes four phases of
knowledge creation: Socialization, Externalization, Combination,
and Internalization. Each phase depends on specific conditions
that remote-first work may interrupt.

This paper develops a theoretical analysis of how remote-first
contexts alter the dynamics of tacit knowledge transfer. We draw
on Theorem-005 (Tacit Knowledge Inexpressibility), which proves
that no finite linguistic representation can capture all
task-relevant tacit knowledge, and on RFC-0007 (Organizational
Amnesia Prevention Protocol), which addresses institutional
knowledge preservation.

Our analysis proceeds in four parts: (1) a review of the tacit
knowledge framework, (2) an analysis of how each SECI phase is
affected by remote-first conditions, (3) a set of propositions
grounded in existing theory, and (4) practical implications for
organizational design.

## 2. Tacit Knowledge Framework

### 2.1 Polanyi's Epistemology

Michael Polanyi's theory of tacit knowledge rests on two
propositions. First, all explicit knowledge is rooted in tacit
commitments: knowing how to use a tool depends on skills that
cannot be fully articulated. Second, tacit knowledge is
constitutively personal: it cannot be transferred by instruction
alone but must be acquired through participation in practice.

This framework implies that knowledge transfer is not merely a
matter of information exchange. When an experienced engineer
guides a junior colleague through a code review, the most
valuable knowledge transmitted is not the rules applied but the
pattern recognition, contextual judgment, and aesthetic sense
that inform which rules matter in this particular case.

### 2.2 The SECI Model

Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) proposed four modes of knowledge
conversion:

- **Socialization**: Tacit to tacit. Knowledge transfer through
  shared experience, observation, imitation, and practice. This
  is the apprenticeship model: learning by doing alongside
  someone who already knows.

- **Externalization**: Tacit to explicit. Articulation of tacit
  knowledge into concepts, models, and metaphors. This is the
  most difficult and valuable phase, as it makes invisible
  competence visible and shareable.

- **Combination**: Explicit to explicit. Systematization and
  combination of explicit knowledge bodies. This is the domain
  of databases, documents, and knowledge management systems.

- **Internalization**: Explicit to tacit. Embodiment of explicit
  knowledge into practice. Reading a manual is combination;
  developing the intuition to know when to deviate from the
  manual is internalization.

### 2.3 Theorem-005 and Inexpressibility

The Tacit Knowledge Inexpressibility Theorem (Theorem-005)
formalizes Polanyi's intuition: there exists a non-empty set T
of task-relevant knowledge such that no finite linguistic
representation can capture all of T without loss of performative
content.

This theorem has direct implications for remote-first work.
If tacit knowledge resists complete articulation, then no amount
of documentation, video recording, or written communication can
fully substitute for the embodied transfer that occurs through
proximate shared practice. The theorem does not claim that
documentation is useless, only that it is structurally
incomplete.

## 3. Remote Context Analysis

### 3.1 Socialization: Severed and Partially Recoverable

Socialization requires shared physical presence, ambient
awareness, and observational learning. In co-located settings,
tacit knowledge flows through peripheral channels: overhearing
conversations, observing how senior colleagues handle edge cases,
noticing the informal heuristics that never appear in
documentation.

Remote-first environments eliminate most peripheral channels.
Synchronous video interactions capture verbal content but lose
postural cues, ambient context, and the serendipitous proximity
that enables observational learning. Asynchronous text
communication further strips away the embodied dimensions of
knowledge.

[SUPPORTED] The impairment is not total. Structured pairing
programs, virtual co-working sessions, and synchronous
collaboration tools can partially recover socialization
functions. However, the bandwidth of tacit transfer through
these channels is measurably lower than through physical
co-presence, consistent with media richness theory (Daft and
Lengel 1986).

### 3.2 Externalization: Marginally Affected

Externalization, the articulation of tacit knowledge into
explicit form, is less dependent on physical proximity. The
process of writing, diagramming, and modeling can occur
independently of shared space. Remote-first organizations often
produce high-quality documentation precisely because the absence
of ambient communication forces explicit articulation.

However, externalization quality depends on the author's
ability to identify what needs articulation, which is itself
a tacit capacity. In remote contexts, feedback loops that
help authors recognize gaps in their explanations are weaker.
The result may be documentation that appears complete but
contains silent omissions.

### 3.3 Combination: Largely Unaffected

Combination, the systematization of explicit knowledge, is
the phase most amenable to remote execution. Databases,
wikis, version control systems, and knowledge graphs operate
entirely on explicit representations and require no physical
co-presence.

Remote-first organizations typically excel at combination,
producing extensive documentation, searchable archives, and
structured knowledge bases. The risk is that this excellence
creates an illusion of completeness: the organization possesses
rich explicit knowledge and mistakes this for comprehensive
knowledge.

### 3.4 Internalization: Impaired at the Margins

Internalization requires practice in context. Reading a
deployment runbook is explicit knowledge; developing the
intuition to know which step to skip when the monitoring
dashboard shows an unfamiliar pattern is internalization.

Remote-first work does not eliminate internalization, but it
changes the context in which practice occurs. Junior members
internalize less ambient knowledge because they have fewer
opportunities to observe expert practice in situ. The
internalization that does occur tends toward the explicit
content available in documentation, producing competent but
less adaptable practitioners.

## 4. Propositions

Based on the analysis above, we advance the following
propositions:

**P1**: Remote-first organizations exhibit a systematic bias
toward explicit knowledge and away from tacit knowledge, with
the bias intensifying over time as the proportion of
proximately transferred tacit knowledge diminishes.

**P2**: The Socialization phase of the SECI model is most
severely impaired by remote-first work, while Combination is
least impaired, creating an asymmetry that favors documented
procedures over embodied judgment.

**P3**: Theorem-005 implies a hard limit on the effectiveness
of documentation as a substitute for proximate tacit transfer:
no amount of explicit knowledge artifact can fully compensate
for the loss of embodied knowledge transfer.

**P4**: Organizations that implement intentional proximate
experience programs (periodic in-person collaboration,
apprenticeship rotations, embedded practice) will exhibit
lower rates of tacit knowledge decay than those relying
exclusively on remote interaction.

**P5**: The knowledge half-life (KHL) metric from RFC-0007
is systematically shorter for tacit knowledge in remote-first
organizations than in co-located organizations, controlling
for turnover rate and organizational complexity.

## 5. Implications

### 5.1 For Organizational Design

Remote-first organizations must resist the temptation to
treat documentation as a complete knowledge strategy. The
SECI model suggests that documentation addresses Combination
and partially supports Externalization, but leaves
Socialization and Internalization underserved.

Organizations should invest in structured proximate
experiences: periodic in-person gatherings, apprenticeship
programs, embedded rotations, and collaborative practice
sessions. These interventions are costly but address the
specific knowledge transfer modes that remote work impairs.

### 5.2 For Knowledge Management Systems

Current knowledge management systems are optimized for
explicit knowledge: search, retrieval, versioning, and
organization. A knowledge management strategy grounded in
the SECI model would additionally track tacit knowledge
indicators: mentoring relationships, observational learning
opportunities, and practice-based competence development.

### 5.3 For Theorem-005 Implications

The inexpressibility theorem implies that even perfect
documentation leaves a residue of inexpressible knowledge.
Organizations must accept this residue as a structural
feature rather than a solvable problem. The goal is not
to eliminate the gap between explicit and tacit knowledge
but to manage it through complementary transfer mechanisms.

### 5.4 Connection to RFC-0007 and RFC-0008

RFC-0008 (Tacit Knowledge Extraction Protocol) was
deprecated precisely because it attempted to treat tacit
knowledge as extractable at a single point in time. The
present analysis confirms this deprecation: tacit knowledge
transfer is an ongoing process embedded in social practice,
not a discrete event amenable to protocol specification.

RFC-0007 (Organizational Amnesia Prevention Protocol)
addresses a related concern: the preservation of institutional
memory through turnover and disruption. Our P5 proposition
extends RFC-0007's Knowledge Half-Life metric to the
remote-first context, predicting accelerated tacit knowledge
decay in distributed organizations.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper develops a theoretical framework for understanding
how tacit knowledge transfer is altered in remote-first
organizations. Using Polanyi's epistemology and Nonaka's SECI
model as foundations, we demonstrate that remote-first contexts
create a systematic bias toward explicit knowledge by
selectively impairing Socialization and Internalization.

Theorem-005 provides the formal grounding: tacit knowledge
resists complete linguistic encoding, which means no
documentation strategy can fully substitute for embodied
transfer. The practical implication is that remote-first
organizations must deliberately design proximate experience
opportunities to maintain tacit knowledge flow.

Future work should empirically test the five propositions
advanced here, particularly P5 regarding knowledge half-life
differential. A quantitative study comparing KHL metrics
across remote-first and co-located organizations would
provide the empirical grounding this theoretical framework
requires.
